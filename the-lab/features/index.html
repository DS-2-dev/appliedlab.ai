A post is the brief students plan from. It describes a problem big enough to change how part of an organization works, says what success looks like, and gives students enough to start without a meeting. A faculty Representative reviews every post before it goes live.

What a post needs to do

There's no fixed order and no form to fill in line by line. Partners write the post their own way, and the Workspace shows five goals beside it. A post is ready for review when it does all five.

  1. Explain the organization and the problem: how the work in question happens today, who is involved, and what the problem costs, written for someone who has never been inside the organization.
  2. Be worth a semester: a problem whose answer changes how part of the organization works. Recurring tasks and one-off chores aren't a fit.
  3. Define success: what result counts as solved, how it will be measured or judged, and by whom. Students' plans and finished work are reviewed against this definition.
  4. Give students something to work from: the data, documents and systems involved and what they look like, the people and time available, and the rules for using them, including what may not leave the organization.
  5. Say what's unknown: open questions are expected, and naming them lets students plan around them.

Everything in a post, and everything shared with its projects, may be processed by AI tools, including tools the Lab doesn't control: students choose their own, though the Lab provides and recommends Claude. Partners share only what their organization allows, and confirm this when they submit.

The post is a title and one open text, written in whatever structure suits the problem. It saves as it's typed, so a draft can be left and finished later. Categories become Notice Board filters, and the company is filled in from the partner's account.

Reviewed before anyone sees it

Once a partner has started a draft, they can ask their Representative for help from the post itself. The question goes to Messages with the draft linked, and the conversation carries on there.

A submitted post waits as Pending for the organization's Representative, a faculty member who covers it. While a post is Pending, the partner can pull it back to Draft and keep editing. The Representative approves the post when a student could write a full action plan from it without a meeting, and otherwise returns it with specific questions.

An approved post goes live on the Notice Board, and students are notified that a new problem is up. A returned post goes back to Draft with the Representative's notes beside it.

The partner stays in control after approval. A live post can be taken off the board and put back later; projects already under the post continue either way. Changes to a live post go to the Representative before they replace what students see, so a student who claimed the original never finds the problem quietly rewritten.

Why a post is thorough

The Lab takes on broad, ambitious problems rather than small one-off tasks, and students can only plan against what the post tells them. The definition of success is what every plan and every submission is measured against, so the care that goes into a post carries through the whole project.

A post doesn't have to hand over internal data. A clear description of what the data looks like is enough for students to build realistic stand-in data, and the differences are reconciled with the real data if the partner later adopts the work.

What it gives each person

Businesses

A way to see what students can do with AI in their field, with a faculty member between them and the work, and a Representative who can help with a draft before it's submitted.

Students

Problems from organizations that actually have them, described in the organization's own words and checked by faculty before they appear.

Faculty Representatives

An early look at what organizations in their area need, which is where the Lab's link between coursework and industry begins.

A problem becomes a project when a student claims it. The action plan that follows is the first work the Lab reviews, and the record the rest of the project is measured against.

Claiming a problem

The Notice Board lists the posts that are live. Students filter the board by field, company and major, and each post shows how many students have claimed it.

Several students can claim the same post, and each claim becomes a separate project. A claim doesn't lock other students out, so claiming carries little risk. A student can claim alone or for a team. A team claim files the project under the team, and only the people named on the project count as working on it. A student with an idea of their own can start a project without a post, and that project moves through the same steps.

When a student claims a post, the platform creates the project with an empty action plan and timeline, notifies the business and its Representative, and updates the Notice Board for everyone viewing it.

Writing the plan

The action plan describes how the student will solve the problem. It has no set structure: students write it their own way, and the Workspace shows seven goals beside it. A plan shows the student understands the problem beyond what the post says, turns the post's definition of success into measures with a starting point to beat, and decides what result means it worked before building anything. It lays out the path and what's left out, names the data, names the risks, and says how AI will be used and what the student will check themselves.

Plans are thorough, and we recommend at least 800 words. The Lab provides Claude through its team account and recommends it, though students choose their own tools. From the plan on, students should be ready to explain and defend anything they write. Synthetic data is fine when it reflects what the partner described. Students write the plan in the Lab's editor, link a Google Doc, or upload a file.

The timeline sits beside the plan. Milestones are grouped into phases and carry start and end dates. Milestones can depend on each other, so moving one moves the milestones that depend on it, and the timeline's history records each change and its reason.

In a plan written in the Lab's editor, a student can select a passage and link it to the milestone that passage describes. The milestone then appears next to the passage, so a reader sees what the student intends to do and when the work is due in the same place.

Why planning comes first

The Lab teaches from the end backward. A student decides what the finished tool has to do, then works back into the skills needed to build it. Writing the plan is where that work starts, before any code or prompts.

Planning first also protects the business. A weak first plan costs the student a revision instead of costing the business time.

Confusion is the expected starting state, and the action plan is how you think your way out.

Review and approval

When the plan is ready, the student submits it, and the plan and its milestones go to the Representative's Approval Hub for review.

An approved plan makes the project Active. The project's messages and files open, and the students and the business are notified. A returned plan comes back to the students with the Representative's feedback, and the project stays in Claimed until they resubmit.

Less back-and-forth

  • Reviewers read one document. Linked passages bring the timeline into the plan, so a Representative can judge whether the dates are realistic without cross-referencing.
  • Businesses see the work start. Each project appears under its post in the business's Pipeline from the day a student claims it.
  • Changes are easy to check. After approval, the timeline can be compared against the approved version.

What it gives each person

Students

A plan they own, a timeline that breaks a large, ambiguous problem into dated steps, and feedback that names what to fix. A student with an active project also gets a Claude account funded by the Lab, which they keep by attending at least 60% of meetings each semester.

Faculty Representatives

A view of how students approach a problem before the work starts, and a chance to shape that approach early. What Representatives see in these plans carries back into their courses.

Businesses

A clear view of who is working on their problem and how the students intend to solve it, before any work reaches them.

Confidence, not pressure

The design keeps the stakes of trying low. A returned plan arrives with the gap named, which reads as a next step rather than a verdict. Dated milestones replace one large, intimidating goal with work a student can finish one piece at a time.

The Lab wants students to attempt work outside their skill sets, fail safely and try again. The claim and the plan review are built for that.

A shared starting point

The plan is the first document a student, a Representative and a business all read. The plan describes the work in terms the business recognizes, gives the Representative something concrete to guide, and gives a team one shared definition of done.

Over a semester, a student's approved plans and timelines show how they think: how they break a problem down, how realistic their estimates are and how they respond to feedback. That record is part of what the Lab offers in place of a line on a résumé.

After approval, the work moves milestone by milestone. Students log progress as they go, and the platform asks about each milestone when its date arrives.

The progress log

The project dashboard keeps a log of the work. Each entry is a short note on what was done, tagged with the milestone it moves forward, and an entry can carry a screenshot or a link. Teammates' entries collect in the same log, so the whole team sees the same account of the project.

When a student marks a milestone complete, the timeline updates and the business can follow along. The business sees completed milestones and logged progress under its post.

Check-ins

When a milestone's end date arrives, the platform asks the students on the project one question: is this milestone done? The question appears on the project's dashboard and in their notifications.

  • Yes, it's done. The student gives the day it was finished, and the milestone is marked complete.
  • Not yet. The student picks a new end date, and dependent milestones shift with it.

The platform asks once per due date. If the end date moves, the question comes back when the new date arrives.

Why the platform asks

Projects usually fail quietly. A student falls behind, says nothing, and the gap grows until the project is past saving. The Lab expects the opposite: report where the work stands, especially when it's late.

File it late anyway, and say what stalled. A leaky ship beats no ship.

A check-in turns reporting into a habit. The question is neutral, the answer takes a moment, and a "not yet" doesn't flag the student as failing. The answer updates the timeline, so the plan keeps matching reality.

Working as a team

Students on a project share one dashboard, and a team's chat can post an update when a milestone or a review changes.

What it gives each person

Students

A clear next step, a record of what they've done, and a way to say they're behind that doesn't feel like an admission of failure.

Faculty Representatives

An accurate view of each project's pace, and an early sign when a project needs help.

Businesses

Steady evidence that the work is moving against the approved milestones.

Finishing the work

When the milestones are done, the students submit their final work for the Representative's review.

A business's Pipeline shows each of its posts with the projects under it, what's waiting on the business, and the work that has been delivered.

Following the work

Each post expands to show the students on each project, its status and its milestone progress. A filter shows only what's waiting on the business, and the list can be sorted to put those items first. Opening a project shows its dashboard.

Delivery

The business is notified when approved work is delivered, and from there can request a meeting with the students or adopt the project.

The students own what they build. A business that wants to use a tool makes an agreement with the students who built it.

Adopting a project

Adopting a project starts a second stage, run directly between the business and the students, in which the roles reverse:

  1. The students sign an agreement with the business.
  2. The business sends a plan and milestones for the next phase.
  3. The students approve the plan or return it with requested changes.
  4. The students work through the milestones, with the same progress log and check-ins as before.
  5. The students submit a final report, and the business accepts it to complete the project or returns it with feedback.

Honest feedback helps

A delivered tool sometimes falls short of what a business hoped for. The Lab asks businesses to say so.

Say what is missing against the milestones you set, and give them the iteration. That is where the learning is.

A business that accepts weak work politely and shelves it leaves the students thinking they succeeded. Specific feedback improves both the tool and the students' next attempt.

What it gives each person

Businesses

One view of their problems and the work on them, and a path from a first project to ongoing work.

Students

A route from a reviewed project to work inside a real organization, earned through work the business has already seen.

Faculty Representatives

A notification when a project is adopted.

A finished project becomes a public story, written by the student who built it and published under their name.

Writing the story

The project's Showcase tab opens once the first stage is complete and stays open through the second. The student drafts the story there: the tool and what it does, the business and the college, screenshots, and a full account of the project.

Before it goes public

Before sending a story for publication, the student removes anything sensitive and replaces it with sample data. An Admin then reviews and approves the draft before it is published. A returned draft comes back with the Admin's feedback.

Where stories appear

Published stories appear on the Stories page and on Home, and each student decides whether their name links to a public profile. The Stories page also carries Lab news and meeting recaps written by Admins.

Why the story matters

A grade says little about how someone works. A story shows a real problem, the choices a student made and how the student handled what went wrong.

Take it apart until you can explain why it is right and where a person still has to be.

Writing the story is the last test of understanding. A student who can explain the tool in public understands it.

What it gives each person

Students

Public work with their name on it, which anyone, including recruiters and businesses, can read.

Businesses

Credit as the organization that brought the problem, and examples to share when they consider the next one.

The Lab

A growing public record of what students build, which brings in the next business and the next student.

Representatives review three things in the Lab's process: a post before it goes live, a plan before work begins, and finished work before it reaches a business. The Approval Hub puts those reviews in one list.

One list, oldest first

The Approval Hub lists the reviews waiting on a Representative: new posts, changes to live posts, action plans and final submissions. The list is sorted so that the item waiting longest comes first, and a filter narrows it to one kind of review. A business's first post is marked.

Decisions on a snapshot

When someone submits a post, a plan or final work, the platform saves a copy of it exactly as sent. The Representative reviews that copy, called a snapshot. Edits made after the submission don't change what's under review, and the project's review history keeps each snapshot, so the people on the project can see later what was approved.

The Representative makes one of two decisions: approve, or return with feedback. The person who submitted is notified either way.

Beside each decision, the Approval Hub shows what that review checks. A post is ready when a student could write a full action plan from it without a meeting. A plan is ready when the Representative would hold the student to it. Finished work is ready when every part of the post's definition of success is answered with evidence the student can defend. The Representative may question any claim, in writing or in a meeting, and work the student can't explain or defend is returned.

Candor with care

The Lab asks Representatives to be direct about the work and generous toward the person. A thin plan from a keen student is the common test.

Send it back naming the specific gap, and describe what a realistic plan would cover.

A return with specific feedback is a normal step in the process.

Partners and coverage

Each Representative covers a set of organizations. People lists them under Partners, with each organization's people, its posts and a place for notes. Every Representative can read those notes, so a partner relationship continues when it passes from one Representative to another.

What it gives each person

Faculty Representatives

One place to see what's waiting, and a clear record of each decision.

Students

A faculty reader for their plans and submissions.

Businesses

A faculty member who approves a problem before students see it, and approves work before the business receives it.

Accounts are set up before anyone signs in, organizations share their work among their own people, and Representatives can bring others in to work with them.

Added ahead of time

An Admin adds people by name and email, alone or as a pasted list, and sets each one's role, organization and access. Each person gets an email from the Lab. Opening it signs them in with Google, with their address already chosen, or with a code sent to that address, and takes them straight to the Workspace with nothing to fill in. The link only says which account is being opened, so a forwarded invitation is no use to anyone else.

Organizations share their work

Everyone in an organization sees its posts and the students' work on them. Each person has one of three access levels: a lead adds and removes the organization's people and sets what each can do, someone who can post writes and sends posts, and someone who can view only looks. An organization always keeps at least one lead.

A person who names an organization when they sign up sees only their own posts until its lead, or an Admin, gives them access. Typing a company's name never opens its posts.

Representatives' teams

A Representative can add colleagues or graduate assistants to work with them across the partners they cover. Someone who reviews with the Representative decides posts, plans and submissions as the Representative would; someone who can view reads them without deciding.

One place for everyone

Admins see People as organizations, each opening to its people with its Representative and notes; Representatives, each opening to their team; members; and requests waiting on a decision. A lead sees their own organization, and a Representative their team and the partners they cover.

Conversations happen in two places: direct messages between people, and team chats for groups working together.

Direct messages and teams

A direct message might connect a student with their Representative, or a business with the students on its project. A team is a standing group that admits new members on request, and its managers can post announcements.

Conversations can be archived to keep the list short. People can leave a team, and a conversation can be deleted when it's no longer needed.

Updates in the team's chat

A team can choose to have its chat post an update when one of its projects completes a milestone, has a milestone come due or run late, or has a plan or submission approved or returned. Each kind of update is turned on or off separately.

Why chat sits next to the work

Many of the Lab's students commute, and the time they can spend together is limited. Keeping the conversation next to the work means a question doesn't wait for the next meeting, and a team stays connected between meetings.

Posts, plans, reports and stories are all written in one editor, which several people can use at the same time.

One editor

The editor handles headings, lists, tables, images, code and pull quotes, and typing a slash opens a menu of everything that can be inserted. Teammates see each other's cursors as they edit. People can comment on a passage, reply in a thread and mention someone, and the person mentioned is notified.

Other sources

A document can also be a linked Google Doc or an uploaded file.

The Lab's Google Drive

Once an Admin connects the Lab's Google account, each project's files are also kept in a folder for it in the Lab's Google Drive, shared with the people on the project, and new Google Docs and Sheets can be made from the project's Files. The business sees the files the project's lead hasn't hidden from it.

History and export

The platform keeps earlier versions of each document, and a document can be exported to Word or PDF.

Lab meetings open with a short session for everyone, followed by workshops. Anyone can come without signing up.

The schedule

Admins set the semester's meetings as a schedule, then name the topic of a single meeting, move it, or cancel it with a reason. The next meeting appears on Home and on the Meet page. When a meeting is cancelled, the reason appears there, along with the next meeting that's still on.

Calendars and reminders

People can add one meeting to their calendar or subscribe to the whole semester, and a subscribed calendar follows changes to the schedule. On the Meet page, visitors can leave an email address for a reminder the day before each meeting, and each reminder includes an unsubscribe link. Members can turn on the same reminder as a notification.

Why there's no sign-up

Everything is show-up.

Meetings, workshops, socials and the showcase require no registration. The reminder list is only a reminder, not a guest list.
